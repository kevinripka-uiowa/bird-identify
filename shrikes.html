Loggerhead and Northern Shrikes

Both are gray, black and white predatory songbirds that hunt from wires and hedgerows in open country, fly with an undulating flight and swoop up to a perch. In Iowa their seasons only overlap in spring and fall, plus the odd wintering Loggerhead, so the date does a lot of the work. When it doesn’t, read the face: the mask, the eye and the bill. Every mark has exceptions, so stack several. Try the Shrike Picker to work through a bird in the field.

Who to expect

Status codes follow the Iowa Ornithologists’ Union checklist. Conservation status is from the Iowa Wildlife Action Plan (2025).

  • Loggerhead ShrikeRegular; nestsScattered statewide in grassland with shrubs, pasture, hedgerows and savanna. A Species of Greatest Conservation Need in Iowa, listed as of special concern. Mostly here about March to Octobercheck; a few may wintercheck.
  • Northern ShrikeRegular; winterComes south from the boreal forest and tundra. Iowa’s extreme dates are 1 October (1989) and 13 April (2013). More common in irruption years. Also a Species of Greatest Conservation Need as a non-breeding bird.

Pitfalls

  • A pale bill base isn’t proof.Some young Loggerheads keep a pale base into winter.
  • No barring isn’t proof either.By late winter, Northerns can lose their barring.
  • Lighting changes the gray.Bright sun can make a Loggerhead look as pale as a Northern; judge the back color only in good light.
  • Great Plains Loggerheads are paler.Western and Great Plains birds are paler above, with white on the rump and scapulars, so a pale rump alone doesn’t mean Northern.
  • Shape and size overlap.Northern is nearly robin-sized and Loggerhead somewhat smaller (James gives wings of 108–120 mm and 91–102 mm), but size only works with both together. Loggerheads tend to look big-headed and slim, Northerns rounder with a longer tail.
  • Northerns can have pale covert edges.Adult Northerns may show light edgings on the wing coverts, unlike adult Loggerheads, though wear can hide them.

Young birds

Juvenile Loggerheads are only around in summer: they molt into adult-like plumage by the end of summer. First-winter Northerns arrive in October and stay brownish most of the winter. So the date usually settles it, and any brownish shrike outside summer is a Northern.

Pitfalls

  • Faint barring on a Loggerhead.Loggerheads in fall and early winter can show some barring, though never as bold as a Northern’s.
  • Young Northerns lose the brown.By late winter and spring they grayer up and can look much more like adults.

Iowa timing

  • Northern Shrike: earliest fall dates 1 Oct 1989, 2 Oct 1970 and 9 Oct 2000; latest spring dates 13 Apr 2013, 12 Apr 2014 and 8 Apr 2018 (IOU early and late dates).
  • Loggerhead Shrike: nests scattered statewide; most arrive in spring and leave in fallcheck. In Missouri, fall migration runs late September to mid-November and birds are back on territory by late March.
  • Overlap: October–November and March–April, when both can be present. In Ontario, James found Loggerheads mostly gone by mid-September and Northerns rarely arriving before late October.
