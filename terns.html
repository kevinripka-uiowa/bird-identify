Iowa Terns: Common, Forster’s and Arctic

Three medium-sized terns with black caps and gray backs. Forster’s and Common are both regular in Iowa; Arctic is a rarity with 6 accepted records. Work out the season and age first, then use the wingtip, underwing and tail.

Who to expect

Status follows Iowa Bird Records and the Iowa Ornithologists’ Union. How common Forster’s is compared with Common in Iowa wasn’t confirmed from an Iowa source;check check eBird for your county.

  • Forster’sRegular; nestsExtreme dates 2 Apr to 9 Nov. Marshes and big wetlands; nests on muskrat houses and floating mats. The only one of the three that winters in the US.
  • CommonRegular migrantExtreme dates 14 Apr to 3 Nov. Large lakes and reservoirs, often with Forster’s.
  • ArcticAccidental6 accepted records, 5 of them August–November; Saylorville has 4. Inland fall strays are mostly young birds. A photo is close to essential.

Molt is a clue too. Common Terns start replacing their flight feathers before fall migration, so a tern with worn dark outer primaries next to fresh pale inner ones, or a gap in the wing, is almost always a Common. Arctic doesn’t molt on migration, so its wing looks evenly colored.

Pitfalls

  • Orange vs. red is hard.Bill color shifts with light and season, and some Commons lose the black tip. Use the wingtip, underwing and tail too.
  • A crossed tail fools you.With the tail tightly closed and the outer feathers crossed, Forster’s white outer edges can hide.
  • Young Forster’s look dark-winged.A 1st- or 2nd-summer Forster’s can show dark outer primaries like a Common’s wedge. Check the head and tail.
  • Be cautious with Arctic.Iowa’s 2017 report wasn’t accepted because it didn’t rule out Forster’s and Common. Get the underwing, bill, legs and tail length, ideally in photos.

Pitfalls

  • Late-summer adults are mixed.An adult can have a partly white forehead and a red bill in August. Go by the wing and tail as on the breeding tab.
  • 1st-year birds look like fall adults.Common and Arctic can take until their 3rd year to look fully adult, so a capless tern in summer isn’t necessarily in fall plumage.

Juvenile (July–October)

Juveniles have short tails and some brown or dark edging on the back. Most Iowa Arctic records fit this season.

Pitfalls

  • Leg color overlaps.Juvenile leg colors are all pale and vary; use them only as support.
  • Watch the inner wing in flight.A white rear edge on the inner wing (Arctic) vs. a dark one (Common) is the best single mark, and it shows well in flight photos.

Iowa records

Extreme dates from the IOU early and late dates list; typical peak migration windows weren’t confirmed.check

  • Common: earliest 14 Apr 1952, 17 Apr 1997, 18 Apr 1986; latest 27 Oct 1985, 27 Oct 2019, 3 Nov 2009.
  • Forster’s: earliest 2 Apr 2006, 2 Apr 2017, 5 Apr 2008; latest 6 Nov 2003, 9 Nov 1977, 9 Nov 2009.

Arctic Tern: 6 accepted records

  • 18 Aug 1999, Saylorville Reservoir, Polk Co. (photo; first state record)
  • 19 Aug 1999, Saylorville Reservoir, Polk Co.check
  • 20–29 Nov 2010, Lake Macbride, Johnson Co. (photo)
  • 22 Sep 2012, Sandpiper Beach, Saylorville Reservoir (photo)
  • 30 Oct 2016, Spirit Lake (The Grade), Dickinson Co.
  • 28 Jun 2026, Saylorville Reservoir (Oak Grove), Polk Co. (photo). Observers debated Forster’s because of its orange-looking, large bill.check

Not accepted: 8 May 2017, Lost Island Lake, Palo Alto Co. The description didn’t rule out Forster’s or Common.
