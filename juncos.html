Iowa Dark-eyed Juncos

Most Iowa juncos are Slate-colored, but not every non-Oregon bird is: Cassiar-type birds (Slate-colored × Oregon intergrades) are probably a regular part of Iowa’s winter flocks, though most can’t be told apart in the field.

How to use it

Work through three checks, in this order, on any odd junco. Tap a check to see where to look on the bird.

Checks run from most to least useful.

Adult males are the easiest to sort. Females and first-winter birds are browner and duller in every form, so many can only be called “Dark-eyed Junco”. That’s a correct ID, not a failure.

Pitfalls

  • Brown Slate-colored females look Oregon-ish.Check the hood border. On Slate-colored the lower edge is smudgy and concave (curving up into the breast at the center) and bleeds into the flanks; on Oregon it is crisp and convex (bulging down). Some sources, including eBird Northwest, use these two words the other way round, so go by the shape.
  • Cassiar is a sliding scale, not a clean form.It is widely considered a Slate-colored × Oregon hybrid swarm, so expect every in-between. eBird has an entry for these birds: “Dark-eyed Junco (Slate-colored x Oregon or cismontanus)”.
  • One wingbar isn’t White-winged.Some Slate-colored birds show thin white wingbars (Sibley: about 1 in 200; older studies found more). Require pale overall gray, larger size, a bigger bill and extra white in the tail too.
  • Light changes everything.checkGray can look brown in low or warm light. Compare birds side by side in the same flock before calling one odd.
  • Juveniles are streaky.In summer, a streaked bird with white outer tail feathers is a young junco; it usually can’t be assigned to a group.
