Sharp-tailed and Pectoral Sandpipers

Pectoral Sandpiper passes through Iowa by the thousands; Sharp-tailed, a Siberian breeder, has five accepted Iowa records, four of them fall juveniles. A juvenile Sharp-tailed hides in Pectoral flocks in September and October. The breast settles most birds: where the streaks stop and how the color fades. Then check the cap and eyebrow. Who to expect

  • Pectoral SandpiperCommon migrantLate March to late May, and early July to late October; fall flocks can reach thousands in late July and August. Juveniles dominate from September.
  • Sharp-tailed SandpiperAccidentalFive accepted records: 3 Oct 1974 and 14 Oct 1990, Coralville Reservoir; 30 Sep 1988, Credit Island, Davenport; 15 May 1994, Riverton WA; 10 Nov 2013, Otter Creek Marsh (photographed). Three more weren’t accepted.

Pitfalls

  • Bright Pectorals.Fresh juvenile Pectorals can look warm and buffy. Color alone isn’t enough; check that the streaks stop in a sharp line.
  • Ruff (Reeve).Juvenile Ruffs also lack a sharp breast edge. Ruff is bigger, with larger white ovals on the sides of the rump. It’s casual in Iowa (27 records), far likelier than Sharp-tailed.
  • Cox’s Sandpiper.A Pectoral × Curlew Sandpiper hybrid with a longer, darker, drooping bill, duller legs and more white on the rump. A 1987 Massachusetts juvenile was first called a Pectoral, then a Sharp-tailed.
  • Spring chevrons.Pectorals can show marks back toward the undertail toocheck, so treat flank and undertail chevrons as support.

Iowa timing

  • Pectoral: earliest spring 19 Feb, 27 Feb, 3 Mar; latest fall 1, 2 and 4 Dec (IOU early and late dates).
  • Sharp-tailed: four fall records, 30 Sep to 10 Nov, and one spring record, 15 May.
