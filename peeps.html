Peeps of Iowa

Five small sandpipers pass through Iowa’s mudflats every spring and fall. Plumage changes all year, so start with structure: how far the wingtips reach, the leg color, and the bird’s shape. That sorts any peep into one of three groups, and plumage, call and behavior finish the job. Try the Peep Picker to work through a bird in the field.

Who to expect

All five are migrants; none nest here.check

  • Least SandpiperCommon migrantThe smallest peep, and the one at muddy edges close to vegetation. Yellowish legs.
  • Semipalmated SandpiperCommon migrantThe standard gray-brown peep of open mudflats, often in big flocks in May and August.
  • Baird’s SandpiperRegular migrantA long-winged, buffy peep of drier mud, sod farms and short grass. Commonest in fall.
  • White-rumped SandpiperRegular migrantA long-winged peep that mostly passes late in spring, from mid-May into June. The white rump shows only in flight.
  • Western SandpiperUncommon migrantMostly a fall bird here, outnumbered by Semipalmateds. Check longer-billed, lankier birds.

Step 2: Inside each group

Semipalmated vs. Western

  • Shape.Semipalmated is compact and round, standing fairly upright, with a small head and a thick, “bull” neck. Western is lankier, with longer legs and a bigger head, so it looks front-heavy.
  • Bill.Semipalmated’s is short, straight and blunt, with a slightly swollen tip; Western’s is longer and droops toward a finer tip. Support only: females are longer-billed, and eastern-breeding Semipalmateds have long bills that overlap Western’s.
  • Molt timing.Western adults molt early, and some are fully gray by August, with gaps in their wings from molting flight feathers. Semipalmateds don’t molt their flight feathers in North America and stay brownish; juveniles stay brown into October. Any first-fall standard peep with lots of plain gray is a Western.
  • Call.Semipalmated: a low, rolling chrrk. Western: a high, sharp, slightly whining shik.

White-rumped vs. Baird’s

  • Posture.White-rumped tilts sharply forward while feeding, its rear end seesawing; it has a heavy chest and a blocky head. Baird’s stays level and flat-backed, with a small head and short legs, picking from the surface.
  • Flanks.Streaks on the flanks rule out Baird’s. Breeding White-rumpeds are heavily streaked down the sides.check
  • Habitat and timing.Baird’s likes dry mud, sod farms and short grass and migrates earlier. White-rumped is a late spring migrant, and its juveniles linger into November.
  • Call.White-rumped: very high, sharp, explosive siit, often heard first. Baird’s: a soft, dry crrep.
  • In flight.White-rumped’s clean white band across the base of the tail is unique among peeps. Baird’s rump has a broad dark center that runs onto the tail.check

Pitfalls

  • Bright Semipalmateds.Some spring Semipalmateds show rusty, but it’s mostly on the crown. Western’s rufous is concentrated on the upper scapulars, crown and ear, with arrowheads running down the flanks nearly to the tail.
  • Worn birds in July.Returning adults are faded and patchy. Use structure first.

Non-breeding adults (July–October)

Fall adults are worn, faded or molting, and timing differs by species. Most peeps finish molting on the wintering grounds, so a plain gray peep in Iowa in August is most likely a Western.

Pitfalls

  • Gray doesn’t mean Western by itself.Late-fall Semipalmateds can be grayish. Check the bill, shape and call.
  • Adults vs. juveniles.Worn adults look ragged and uneven; juveniles look neat and evenly fringed. In August both are around.

Immatures (juveniles, August–October)

Fresh juveniles are the common fall peeps, with neat, evenly fringed feathers.check

Pitfalls

  • Juvenile Least vs. Baird’s.Both are buffy-breasted. Least has a white “V” on the back and short wingtips; Baird’s is evenly scaly with long, crossed wingtips.
  • Juvenile Semipalmated vs. Western.Rufous limited to the upper scapulars and a paler face point to Western; anchor-shaped marks on the lower scapulars are a Western mark too. Call a bird a Western only if it’s long-billed and shows some rufous in the upper scapulars. Tawny juvenile Semipalmateds with rufous edges are the trap.

Iowa timing

Rough windowscheck:

  • Least Sandpiper: April–May and July–October; one of the first back in July.
  • Semipalmated Sandpiper: May to early June, and July–September.
  • Baird’s Sandpiper: April–May, and mostly August–October.
  • White-rumped Sandpiper: late May to mid-June; scarcer in fall, when juveniles can linger into November.
  • Western Sandpiper: mostly August–October; scarce in spring.
