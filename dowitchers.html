Iowa Dowitcher Decoder

Short-billed and Long-billed Dowitchers both move through Iowa, and despite the names, bill length barely helps. Voice, the tertials of juveniles and the breast sides of breeding adults do.

How to use it

Work through three checks, in this order, on any dowitcher. Tap a check to see where to look on the bird.

Date is a strong clue in Iowa: from late September on, a dowitcher is most likely Long-billed. Silent nonbreeding birds are hard, and many are best left as “dowitcher sp.” That’s a correct ID, not a failure.

Pitfalls

  • Bill length overlaps.Female Short-billed have long bills and male Long-billed have short ones, so many birds fall in the overlap. Use bill length as support, never alone.
  • Iowa’s Short-billed can be orange to the belly.Many guides picture the Atlantic form, which has a white belly. The interior form that passes through Iowa (hendersoni) is much more orange below, so check how the breast sides are marked, not how far the orange goes.
  • Worn and molting adults lose their patterns.By late summer, adults are faded and patchy, and the fine markings that separate them wear away. Lean on voice and date.
  • Age the bird before comparing.The tertial check only works on juveniles, and the breast-side check only works on breeding adults. Comparing a juvenile of one species to an adult of the other will mislead you.
  • A white belly in spring isn’t proof of Short-billed.Long-billed passing through in spring are often still molting and can show white below. Check the breast sides and listen.
  • Who’s calling matters.A dowitcher calling from the mud while it feeds is more likely Long-billed; Short-billed are mostly quiet on the ground.
  • Light changes everything.Warm, low light makes gray birds look buffy and bright birds look orange. Compare birds side by side in the same flock.
