Orioles of the US

Adult males are easy. Females and young birds are the hard part: work out where you are, then shape, then whether the belly matches the breast, then the face and wings.

How to use it

Work through the checks in order. Tap a check to see where to look on the bird.

Where you are does a lot of the work. In Iowa and the East, a dull oriole is almost always a Baltimore or an Orchard. Bullock’s takes over in the West, with hybrids on the Great Plains. Hooded and Scott’s are Southwest birds, and Audubon’s and Altamira live only in South Texas. Spot-breasted Oriole lives in southeast Florida, and Streak-backed is a rare visitor to Arizona and California.

In Iowa

Baltimore and Orchard both nest in Iowa. The earliest spring arrivals on record are 19 April for both. Orchards leave early, and the latest Orchard on record is 20 October. A few Baltimores have lingered into January and February.check

Three western orioles are accidental. Each record needs a report to the Iowa Ornithologists’ Union:

  • Bullock’s: 9 accepted records, mostly in May (1993, 2016, 2019, two in 2021), plus September (1995, 2008) and winter birds at Elk Horn (December 1999) and Ottumwa (November 2011 to March 2012). No sign of nesting.
  • Hooded: 2 accepted records: Fairbank, 25 May 2003, and Ames, 9–10 May 2014.
  • Scott’s: 2 accepted records: Estherville, 7–16 April 2008, and Lake City, 12 December 2025.

Spring is when strays show up here. A dull “Baltimore” in May with a whitish belly and a dark eyeline is worth a closer look and photos.

Pitfalls

  • Two kinds of black-bibbed young males.First-year male Orchard and Hooded both look like a female with a black bib. Hooded is bigger, with a longer tail, a longer curved bill and thinner wingbars.
  • Pale young Baltimores.Young female Baltimores can be pale, with only a yellow wash and a grayish back. Check the belly against the breast and look for an eyeline before calling it a Bullock’s.
  • Some birds can’t be named.Some female Bullock’s and Baltimores aren’t safe to identify, and hybrids on the Great Plains fall in between: brighter than a Bullock’s, duller than a Baltimore.
  • Altamira or Hooded.Both are orange with black bibs in South Texas. Altamira is much bigger, with a thick, straighter bill, an orange shoulder and black only in front of the eye. Young Altamiras lack the orange shoulder, so use size and bill.
  • Juvenile Audubon’s has no hood.It’s a plain olive-yellow bird with a green back; use the long tail, the thick bill and the dense-brush habits.
  • Scott’s or Hooded.Female Scott’s has a gray-green head, a streaked back, broader wingbars and often black flecks on the face. Female Hooded is plain-backed with thin wingbars.
  • Orchard leaves early.Orchards start heading south in late July. A dull oriole in Iowa in September is more likely a young Baltimore.check
  • Streak-backed in the Southwest.An orange oriole with a streaked back (not black) in Arizona or California is a rare Streak-backed, not a Hooded.

To check

  • Bills.Whether Baltimore, Hooded and Altamira have a blue-gray base to the lower bill. Sources also differ on whether Orchard’s bill is straight or slightly curved, and on the shape of Scott’s bill.
  • Young Hooded.Juvenile and first-year female Hooded details, and the tail color of female Orchard and Hooded.
  • Adult male details.Baltimore’s orange shoulder and Bullock’s tail pattern weren’t confirmed.
  • Young Audubon’s and Altamira.When young Audubon’s get the hood, and what first-year Altamiras look like.
  • Migration.Whether Orchards also go around the Gulf, whether Bullock’s stops in northwest Mexico to molt, and whether Bullock’s and Hooded winter in South Texas.
  • Florida and Arizona specials.The current status of Spot-breasted in Florida, and the seasons of Streak-backed records.
  • Iowa.Typical arrival and departure dates and how common Orchard is. Three older Bullock’s reports (1981, 1987, 1992) have unknown outcomes.
  • Hybrids.Specific field marks of Baltimore × Bullock’s hybrids.
