Trumpeter and Tundra Swans

Both are big, white and black-billed, and in Iowa both turn up in fall, winter and spring. No single mark works on every bird, so stack several: voice, how the black skin meets the eye, the shape of the forehead and the yellow spot carry the most weight. Age the bird first, because young birds use different marks. Try the Swan Picker to work through a bird in the field.

Who to expect

  • Trumpeter SwanRegular; nests; all yearRestored by the Iowa DNR from 1995 on, with 1,218 birds released through 2022. The DNR counted a record 158 nests in 2025. Pairs and families use marshes, ponds and prairie wetlands; many stay all winter on open water. Iowa birds often carry neck collars or wing tags.
  • Tundra SwanRegular; migrantPasses through about March–April and late October–Decembercheck, often in big, noisy flocks. About a quarter of the eastern population stops on the Upper Mississippi River in fall. Doesn’t summer in Iowa.
  • Mute SwanFeral; escapesOrange bill with a black knob at the base, and an S-curved neck. Not covered here: the bill rules it out at a glance.

Step 1: Age it, and listen

Tap a check to see where to look.

Start with the color; the bill check just rules out a Mute Swan.

Listen first. Voice is often the easiest mark, and young birds call like adults. Trumpeter: a deep, brassy honk, like a short trumpet toot, often in twos or threes. Tundra: a higher, softer “woo-woo” bugling, with crane-like clucking.

Step 2: Identify it

Adults

Both species are all white, sometimes with rusty staining on the head and neck, and both have black legs. Everything comes down to the face, the bill and the voice.

Pitfalls

  • No yellow doesn’t mean Trumpeter.About 1 in 10 Tundras has no yellow spot. Check the eye and the forehead.
  • A V forehead isn’t proof either.Some Tundras show a V. Trumpeters always do, so a U points firmly to Tundra.
  • Size fools people.A lone swan has nothing to compare against, and the two species overlap.
  • Lighting and angle.Side-on, the thin black line on a Tundra can merge with the eye. Wait for a head-on look.

Young birds (first fall to spring)

Judge the plumage by the date: both start gray-brown, but Tundras whiten much sooner. The forehead doesn’t help, because young Tundras often show a V, and young swans’ heads and bills look swollen, so shape doesn’t help yet.

Pitfalls

  • Bills vary.Young birds of the same species and age can have quite different bills. Weigh plumage and legs more.
  • Young Trumpeters may still be growing.They may not reach full size until their second fall, so compare young birds with young birds.
  • White young Trumpeters.A few Trumpeter cygnets are white; check the voice and the face before calling a white young bird a Tundra.

Iowa timing

  • Trumpeter: all year. Nests from about April; families stay together through the first winter. The 2025 midwinter survey counted over 4,700 swans.
  • Tundra: spring about March–April; fall about late October–Decembercheck. Most numerous along the Mississippi River; about 25% of the eastern population uses the Upper Mississippi in fall (USGS).
  • May through September: a swan in Iowa is almost certainly a Trumpeter (or a Mute Swan).
